# How I work

## People first. Then the tool.
1. Map how the team really works: who does what, where time is lost, which spreadsheet everyone secretly depends on.
2. Build the smallest useful tool. Not the most impressive one, the one volunteers will open on a Tuesday night.
3. Hand it over: templates, a playbook, documentation. If it stops working the day I leave, I failed.

## The three laws of my tools (after Isaac Asimov, I, Robot)
1. A tool may not harm the team or, through inaction, allow it to lose time.
2. A tool must obey the volunteers who use it, except where that would conflict with the First Law.
3. A tool must outlive my internship, as long as that does not conflict with the First or Second Law.

## On AI specifically
- Grounded over impressive: my AI tools cite their sources (page, section) and stay read-only when they touch important work.
- Local first when it matters: transcription and search run on my own Mac, so audio and documents never leave it, and it costs ≈ €0.
- Plain files: Markdown, CSL-JSON, Zotero. No lock-in.
- I direct AI coding agents for building, and I check everything myself (visual QA, tests, deployment).

## Why sport organisations
Small clubs and associations run on volunteers with little time and money. That is exactly where a well-chosen tool changes the most. I saw it from the inside in every club I worked with, and my thesis studies it.

## What I bring to a team
- Someone who understands the field (matchday, sponsors, volunteers) and the tools (AI, automation, web).
- A translator between the people and the tech.
- Discipline and calm from the Navy reserve.
